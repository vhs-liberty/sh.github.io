## [The Piece](https://quantified-linkedin.carrd.co/)

*Introduction*

LinkedIn is important for work development but many people, me included, find that sometimes its algorithm makes suggestions that simply do not add up. In my piece, I wanted to present the identity LinkedIn uses for advertising purposes, my advertising identity, in a means towards quantifying and regaining control of my LinkedIn-self. Presented as a singular webpage, inspired by LinkedIn’s very platform, the user ends up scrolling from the end to the beginning upon first loading. This has to do with how a user account’s posts are featured in such a reverse chronological order. Hereby, the piece itself will be explored critically through Haggerty and Ericson’s “The surveillant assemblage” and Gillespie’s “The politics of ‘platforms’” after a short creative reflection.

*Creative Reflection*

The first conceptualization of this project was as a short book titled “My LinkedIn Story” where I give shape and space to who LinkedIn thinks I am based on the data it has. A lot of that data is things I willingly gave up when building my profile to have better professional opportunities. However, some of these are things it stored of its own merit or policy (LinkedIn). I first intended to clean it all out into a neat table and then plotting it out into a scrollable book but upon realizing that a lot of the data is simply things stored from my profile I chose to focus on something that is not on there. Enter the “ad_targetting.csv” file, which I found very interesting as it explained the odd notifications I receive all the time. Still thinking of the book idea, I chose to visualize it into a singular webpage that looks like a platform scroll-through, where a lot of the artistic decisions were made. This is because it would give an easier flow and that webpages can behave like a book depending on design. The dashed lines were chosen because their lack of solidness indicates the incorrect profiling; the font, Arial, is to do with its popularity on such platforms; the ellipsis to indicate uncertainty and generate eeriness or discomfort; and the rhetoric designed to satirize my identity as given by LinkedIn. Colors and background choices were to do with the look of blank paper to represent documents such as resumes or cover letters utilized in the construction of the professional identity.

*Critical Exploration*

This identity branches off into the online world through platforms like LinkedIn here. In “The surveillant assemblage” is the idea of the “data-double”, a “new type of body” created from the act of surveillance (Haggerty and Ericson 613). Though the LinkedIn presented self is the professional identity of an individual there is still a “data-double” created for the purposes of advertising, as the satirical rhetoric of my piece aims to show (Haggerty and Ericson). Data that is either wrong or simply fails to properly identify an individual and ends up suggesting things not necessarily in that individual’s interest. This is boldly presented in the questions at the top after the data is shown, such as its categorization of myself as an active member of the platform- “How has it categorized me as a ‘member’ of its platform; the member traits?” The data itself is also a representation of how LinkedIn strips people of anonymity in the name of “trust” but then also raises questions about having so much information out there up for grabs (LinkedIn). The fact that advertisers receive this type of information, and that the algorithm generates some incorrect items too, then just generates another of the “flows of the surveillant assemblage” (Haggerty and Ericson 613).

In the explorations of “platform” by Gillespie in their article “The politics of ‘platforms’” is its semantical origins (Gillespie 349). LinkedIn, of course a platform by the popular understanding of the term, is also a platform in a political manner. That is, in the sense of “issues a political candidate or party endorses as their ‘platform’” because by missing anonymity, people who engage in posts indivertibly reveal their “platform”, their beliefs (Gillespie 350). Beliefs are a component of one’s identity and in the case of LinkedIn’s advertising “data double” they take form as the tracked data (Haggerty and Ericson). That is, the interests, the organizations someone follows, the people someone follows, and so on, altogether building a “platform” for the “data double” wherein advertisers can find the correct audience (Gillespie; Haggerty and Ericson). However, because the data can be erroneous, the audience is not always correct, but it nevertheless fails to provide a “figurative ‘platform’ of opportunity” as Gillespie explains (Gillespie 354).

*Conclusion*

Hence, coming to the end of this statement, I aimed to give form to my LinkedIn-self to quantify and regain control by satirizing and presenting who I am to advertisers.
