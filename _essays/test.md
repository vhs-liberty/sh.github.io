                            The Egg Wars and Blefuscu: A Satiric Description of English Conflicts and French Influence

Johnathan Swift’s satirical art is very prominent across *Gulliver’s Travels* and shifts across each story quite subtly. Focusing on A Voyage to Lilliput, a lot of satire can be seen from the perspective of political conflict, but also on human nature. Perhaps, human nature is most satirised across all four books, and this satirizing is satirised in of itself. Yet here, there will be a greater focus on how this all plays out in A Voyage to Lilliput, with its several uses of satire. Most prominent of them is the “egg war”, a clear criticism on the nature of England’s conflicts of the time, and the references of Blefuscu’s conflicts, where Swift approaches the role of France in England’s conflicts. Another rather important example is the size difference, a showcase of human violence through Gulliver’s thoughts, for example crushing the little Lilliputians or eating one. Hence, such satire, that may be allegorical in nature as well, is used to critic conflict and its causes. Thus, this essay will explore how Swift uses satire in A Voyage to Lilliput to criticise England’s conflicts of the time done through the explicitly detailed “egg wars” and “Blefuscu wars”.

Swift satirises the English conflicts of his time through exaggeration by referencing some of their prominent details in retelling the “egg wars”. Specifically, the lines “six rebellions”, “…fomented by the Monarchs of Blefuscu…”, “…whole Party rendred incapable by Law…”, all may point towards the Jacobite Rebellions or Jacobites Rising as it was a series of revolts that followed the Glorious Revolution and aligns with the timeline of when Swift published the piece (Swift 41, National Army Museum). This may be attributed to how during Swift’s time England’s form of rule was constantly finding ways to change since deposing the Roman Catholic ruler during the Glorious Revolution (National Army Museum). By exaggerating these conflicts, Swift satirises them by making a comedic allegory, wherein he appears to be comparing the English politician’s scoundrels to something as silly as how to break an egg. Here, Swift also touches upon the nit-picking of religious text, when it is stated “…mere Strain upon the Text…That all true Believers shall break their Eggs at the convenient end…”, where everyone appeared to go into war simply due to the question of how to interpret a single line, which comments on the nature of England’s conflicts of the time (Swift 41). Satire here is enforced using exaggeration since the idea of having several wars due to the question of breaking an egg may appear silly, yet such question came to cause some of England’s conflicts at the time, thus the exaggeration acts as a medium to comment on them by making them appear silly, like how to break an egg, when it seems to be up to “…Man’s Conscience…” (Swift 41). Thus, Swift uses exaggeration to satirise the English conflicts, potentially directing it to the Jacobite Rebellions because of the references he uses and retells it all through the lens of conflict over how to break an egg.

The “egg wars” also hold an element of absurdity that strengthens the satire beyond exaggeration and produces a humourist criticism that some conflicts are unnecessary. The idea of bloody conflicts over how to break an egg appears absurd to a reader as it may sound like an unnecessary quarrel, emphasised by the line “…eleven thousand Persons have…suffered Death, rather than submit to break their Eggs at the smaller end.” (Swift 41). The absurd element is portrayed using the word “submit”, perhaps criticising the persistence of the Jacobite Rebellions, which kept coming despite so many shortcomings (National Army Museum). Bringing back the concept of silliness, the idea of so many dead for the sake of breaking an egg may sound shocking to a reader, and this shock is done by the absurd element, which generates the satire by once more being an exaggeration. Together, this is Swift’s satirical twist, and satire is naturally a humourist approach to a sensitive subject. It varies in the “egg wars” here as it not only focuses on England’s conflicts, but also the relatively small religious reasonings that bring them up to begin with. Thus, the “egg wars” are a prominent means of creating satire through absurdity and bringing about Swift’s satirical twist to approaching a sensitive subject, wherein the satire is on England’s conflicts but also the religious quarrels causing them to begin with. 

The “Blefuscu wars” act opposite and are Swift’s satirical approach to the role of France in England’s conflicts of his time, offering a criticism on France’s role during such conflicts. That is, bringing back the Jacobite Rebellions, there were times when France would intervene by providing refuge, which is a contrast with some of the events that occur in the story (National Army Museum). For example, the line “…the Exiles always fled for refuge to that Empire”, may be pointing to the many who fled to France following defeat (Swift 41). Then, there is the potential connection with the Spanish Wars of Succession, as seen in “…a most obstinate War for six and thirty Moons past.” (Swift 40). These connections with France provide a criticism of the complexity of law from the perspective of how prosecution is avoidable by fleeing to an enemy state, as seen with Blefuscu and Gulliver, or in the Jacobite Rebellions. The satire here behaves similarly as in the “egg wars”, and therefore, helps provide a criticism on the interventions of France during England’s conflicts, portrayed by the “Blefuscu wars”. 

To conclude, Swift uses Satire through absurdism, exaggeration, and silliness all explicitly portrayed in the descriptions of the “egg wars” and “Blefuscu wars”. Potentially criticising the Jacobite Rebellions or Risings, and discussing how France has influenced England, Swift’s satirical art is used to make the reader realise how silly all these scoundrels tend to be as the idea of wars over breaking an egg appears random, yet such minute quarrels have been the very reason for some of England’s conflicts during Swift’s time.
