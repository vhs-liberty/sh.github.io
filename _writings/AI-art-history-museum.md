The advancements in AI have necessitated changes in many sectors of the world’s economies. The art world is similarly finding itself having to adapt, with galleries now beginning to include AI art. This can be imagery fully generated by generative AI or be work done in collaboration with AI, including algorithmic creations such as with non-fungible tokens (NFTs) (Goetz, pp. 186 and 188; Smethurst et al., p. 5). NFTs are digital assets “registered on a blockchain” with an electronic or cryptographic signature that differentiates them (Smethurst et al., pp. 4-5). The art world can be said to be more receptive, though not totally, of such art, whilst those outside the art world have been more critical (Smethurst et al.). These outsiders, so-to-say, refers to online and offline art communities who compose what Gregory Sholette titles the “cultural dark matter” of the art world (Sholette, p. 1). To them, art by or in collaboration with AI is not art whatsoever. The logic of this criticism usually falls to two lines of thinking. The first is that AI art composes a form of thievery and the second is what Trystan S. Goetze summarizes as “the result of a mechanical process, with no genuine artistic skill on display” (Goetze, p. 186). It can also be both. These ideas can be read as a divide in an already divided art world, where on one hand, within the art world, people are beginning to buy, trade, and consume AI art in its varying forms, whereas on the other, outside the art world, people are rejecting such technology totally, with others accepting it, but in fewer numbers. To further complicate this then is Andrea Fraser’s theory of art galleries as institutions of critique, whereby what enters the gallery can then definitively be seen as *art* (Fraser).

Andrea Fraser in “From the Critique of Institutions to an Institution of Critique” traces how criticism of institutions became institutionalized, when galleries began taking in work designed to challenge their organizational framework (Fraser). She describes “the institutional critic as a guerilla fighter engaging in acts of subversion and sabotage” (Fraser, p. 102). This form of art, the one that criticizes, that aims to redefine the institution and by extension the art world, is verily what leads towards the idea of what enters the gallery is then art (Fraser). Citing Daniel Buren, Fraser identifies this as what is “‘exhibited as art’...becomes an *ideal-object*, which brings us once again to art’” (Fraser, p. 103). Returning to AI art, this theory aids in seeing how by having it incorporated into the gallery, it gives legitimacy to the idea of AI art as art. Interestingly, such exhibitions have usually been, by critics, described as kitsch (Smethurst, et al. pp., 4-5). Kitsch comes from the German verb verkitschen, meaning “to sell and trade or to make cheaply” (Smethurst, et al., p. 4). Many have theorized how to define kitsch, but its proponent, Clement Greenberg, saw it as mass produced art designed for cultural consumption (Smethurst, et al., p. 4). As part of the institutionalization of critique, institutions have struggled with the question of how to curate and display kitsch (Burcaw 63-92). This struggle also draws from when does kitsch belong in an art gallery compared to a history museum, where it can better aid future historians in understanding the past (Burcaw 63-92).

The issue drawn so far is that AI art displayed in art galleries or the broader art world, to include festivals, biennials, etc., are given legitimacy by virtue of being in the space per the theory of the institutional critic. Then, in so doing, the art world finds itself once again challenged by those it excludes—though again, some in the art world also challenge the idea of exhibiting AI art, particularly by decreeing it as kitsch (Sholette; Smethurst, et al., pp. 4-5). Other criticism, by the excluded particularly, usually falls between thievery and the lack of artistic skill in the process (Goetze, p. 186). This then is a problem that institutions must now tackle, in addition to the problem first posited by the onset of AI: how to curate AI art. Instead, this is better reframed as *should* the art world’s institutions curate AI art, in consideration of it being attached to the debate of if kitsch should be exhibited, and in consideration of the arguments of the excluded. The latter also echoes the greater issue of the exclusiveness found in the art world, which continues to be criticized by contemporary artists (Sholette).

To answer this, one can simply look at how people react when an institution decides to exhibit, or engage in any way, with AI art. This can include the reception of the decision by the Museum of Modern Art to buy 2 NFTs, or more recently, the reception of Nettrice Gaskins’ work in the SFO Museum’s “The Women of Afrofuturism” exhibition (Smethurst, et al., p. 5; Vaziri). The latter will be used as a case study example for this essay’s central argument on should art institutions curate AI art. However, before describing said argument, one more thing needs describing.

Part of the criticism on Nettrice Gaskins’ work is that the art produced by AI, to represent Black women, could instead have been produced by local Black artists, who belong to the “dark matter” of the art world, who then would have had their work exhibited instead (Vaziri; Sholette, p. 1). However, the work itself can still stand as a case study alone. Second, there do exist AI focused institutions, that is, organizations and similar which focus on displaying, exhibiting, and helping so-called AI artists (Iervolino and Milne, pp. 167-172). Their position in this argument is too grand for the scope of the essay, and if anything, they become a more legislative question than anything else. With this aside, then, this essay will argue for why art institutions, specifically art galleries, and thereby, the art world at large, should not display AI art, and rather, that AI art, as kitsch too, should instead be displayed in history museums, by looking at the work of Nettrice Gaskins in “The Women of Afrofuturism” exhibit with the SFO Museum.

    <p> On AI Art </p>
</div>

What is AI Art?

AI art is art produced by “text-to-image generative AI, a computer application that uses machine learning to produce images in response to user-provided text prompts” (Goetz, p. 186). It can also be defined as art that uses generative AI tools “as part of the artistic process” (Iervolino and Milne, p. 163). More specifically, the machines producing these works, or aiding in, are usually trained on mass amounts of data (Goetz, p. 188). This “consist of a very large number of images with accompanying text descriptions” (Goetz, p. 188). Other times, the AI is an algorithmic specific program, as with NFTs, which do belong in part to the domain of AI art (Smethurst et al., p. 5). Due to how expansive this term can be, this essay will use the term “creative AI ecosystem” coined by Serena Iervolino and Alasdair Milne (Iervolino and Milne, p. 167). This ecosystem is one including “an eclectic range of institutional ‘actors’ working autonomously or collaboratively to support artists in delivering public-facing outcomes” (Iervolino and Milne, p. 167). In other words, it is the amalgam of all actors, that is, institutions, organizations, festivals, etc. which focalize upon moving AI art forward, and if anything, grounding its legitimization.

AI Art as Theft

To understand why AI art is difficult to describe as art, in the first case regarding why such work should not be curated, and therefore, not exhibited, is by looking at the arguments on thievery. Goetze in “AI Art is Theft: Labour, Extraction, and Exploitation: Or, On the Dangers of Stochastic Pollocks” gives two strong understandings of AI art as thievery as proclaimed by critics. The first is style theft and the second is labor theft. Style theft refers, plainly, to artists who claim others’ style as their own, where “there is [a] fuzzy line between taking inspiration from another artist’s style and ripping it off” (Goetz, p. 190). Generative AI, by being trained on so many artists’ work, without their consent no less, can, and often does, imitate artistic styles (Goetz, pp. 188 and 190). Goetze gives the example of Polish artist Greg Rutwoski, who following this, saw a “drop in interest in their work-for-hire” (Goetz, p. 190).

> For example, shortly after Stable Diffusion [an AI art platform] was released, its users discovered they could produce high-quality fantasy scenes by adding the name of a Polish artist, Greg Rutwoski, to their text prompts [45]. Stability AI eventually disabled this function with regard to Rutwoski’s work, but only after he publicly objected.
> (Goetz, p. 190)

This issue with style theft is but one, with others including deception, and more dangerously, reputational harm (Goetz, p. 190). However, these applications do not apply to art institutions and will therefore not be discussed. As for labor theft, Goetze applies the understanding by Denis Diderot on intellectual property:

> ...when one creates a piece of creative work...the labour of one’s mind is mixed with the product. And, just as for the products of physical labour, since we own our intellectual labour, we own the creative works that result. An artist owns their art because their creative labour is mixed with the resulting images.
> (Goetz, p.191)

The resources an artist engages include not just their supplies, but also the “language, concepts, ideas, stories, mathematics, culture, artistic processes and principles, and so on” (Goetz, p. 191). Altogether, then, this relates back to what was posited earlier as “the result of a mechanical process, with no genuine artistic skill on display” (Goetz, p. 186). The counter here often is that many AI artists do include some skill, or other process, that is something from the intellectual pool previously mentioned beyond prompt engineering. Yet the issue with this then arises when it is combined with the previously stated arguments. AI art inherently ends up stealing labor or with style imitation, if not direct imitation or copying, which leads to appropriation of another’s mind’s labor that the machine stole. Altogether, the product is, despite whatever modifications, a mimicry of someone else’s creative effort, someone else who never agreed to having AI be trained on their work. One may say that those modifications are the very creative labor, or that prompt engineering is creative labor, but this does not resolve the question of style theft, itself not resolved by stating that artists copy other artists. Artists, if anything, criticize any other artist whose style is not inspiration, but instead imitation. In terms of Gaskins’ work, both arguments are at play. The San Francisco Chronicle records that much of the criticism was in calling the work “AI slop,” a term that usually echoes the style imitation factor, but is also entering the world of kitsch (Vaziri). Gaskins then in fact used the second argument on creative labor and intellectual property to defend the work, stating in the Chronicle that “the backlash reflects widespread misconceptions about how AI-assisted art is created” and “‘artists who use AI are using it as a tool, not to automate or do all the work’” (Vaziri).

AI Art as Kitsch
Kitsch is important towards understanding why AI art should be in history instead of art museums. Kitsch, as defined earlier, is from the German verb verkitschen, meaning “to sell and trade or to make cheaply” (Smethurst, et al., p. 4). George Ellis Burcaw in *Introduction to Museum Work* adds that kitsch is “popular or mass art” (Burcaw, p. 78). Together, the understanding of kitsch, as it was in the 20th century, is art designed cheaply, quickly, and if anything, for decor purposes (Smethurst et al., p. 4). The latter is what Burcaw describes as “hanging on the walls of most homes” (Burcaw, p. 78). In the context of the digital 21st century, “tokenisation allows kitsch collectibles – usually digital, sometimes physical – to be bought and sold online” (Smethurst et al., p. 4). The AI component is in how “NFTs are algorithmically generated avatars and profile pictures” (Smethurst et al., p. 5). This, of course, is different to just generative AI, which functions more so on converting text into images (Goetz, p. 188). Altogether then, NFTs are clearly part of AI art, or better yet, the “creative AI ecosystem” (Iervolino and Milne, p. 167).

With this understanding of kitsch, this essay returns to Gaskins’ work. Regardless of how the work was made, as in by specific algorithmic machines like with NFTs, or generative AI, the idea of kitsch can be applied by extension to how the “creative AI ecosystem” is kitsch (Iervolino and Milne, p. 167). That is to say that, most important to kitsch, is of course the technology to produce en masse and cheaply (Smethurst, et al., p. 4). This was identified by scholars like Greenberg (Smethurst, et al., p. 4). AI, or regular algorithmic machines, do exist cheaply, and as a result, are therefore susceptible to producing en masse. As such, since Gaskins’ work was such a product, it has, if implicit at any rate, connotations of this facet; it is why the reception was so harsh by the public, that yes, there may have been some creative intent or otherwise, but the tool itself is, for a lack of better word, stained with this issue of being a tool for mass and cheap production. This further solidifies the kitsch factor, and thereby, exhibiting AI art, or curating it, shares similarities with curating, or exhibiting, kitsch or “popular art,” to quote Burcaw (Burcaw, p. 78). The issue of kitsch may then best be addressed by way of history museums.

<div class="parth">  
    <p> A New History Museum </p>
</div>

An interesting proposition for curators of history museums is to collect contemporary, present-day objects, that one day future curators would display (Burcaw, pp. 69-70). This is because finding objects from the past can be an expensive hurdle for many history museums, and in the case of digital technologies, it is easy for objects to be lost forever (Burcaw, pp. 69-70). Kitsch’s main issue, if anything, is that many copies of one piece exist, and nothing, really, can distinguish one from another. The question then is, when an *art* gallery takes one in, what is its purpose? Is it there as *art* or just as a means to include the general public? And in so doing, is it all simply to remove the veil of exclusivity and classism usually associated with such institutions? (Sholette). This is part of the issue on how to curate—if it should be curated at all— kitsch art. Yet because kitsch art can also represent aspects of people, for instance, what decor existed in, say, the 2000s, they do hold more historical value than artistic value (Burcaw 63-92). A historian a century from now may appreciate if present-day curators collect kitsch objects, so the historian can more easily study the present-day cultures. This idea can thus be extended to AI art.

AI is, in the end, a technology, regardless of which type, and as with all technologies, each progression is better than the last. In storing the “creative AI ecosystem” in history museums, there is an opportunity to shift the tension from art galleries, and in so doing, an opportunity to redefine the history museum (Iervolino and Milne, p. 167). To understand this, first an idea of a history museum will be developed using George Ellis Burcaw’s *Introduction to Museum Work*. Burcaw suggests that one function of museums is to teach, and with that, uses the term history museum to refer to “museum activity related to discovering, preserving, and interpreting important knowledge about past human behavior” (Burcaw, p. 63). To further contextualize this one can frame it in the theory of the institutional critique. This was a major shift in art institutions predominantly, where they went from institutes of critique, to institutionalizing said critique (Fraser).

An Institutional Shift

In the essay collection *Art and Contemporary Critical Practice: Reinventing Institutional Critique*, Hito Steyerl presents an essay titled “The Institution of Critique,” where she traces the shift from institutes of critique to institutionalized critique in two waves. The first of these was the questioning of “the authoritarian role of the cultural institution” in the 1970s, with the second wave in the 1990s focusing on remaining “institutions that, in their view [that of artists], were still authoritarian” (Steyerl, pp. 14 and 16). This major shift, which, for better or for worse, forever changed the art world at large, is what AI art’s inclusion in history museums is to do. It is to turn the history museum from a collector of past relics, to also a collector *for* the future past, that is, those in the future studying the present-day, which to them, is the past. In so doing, it is to become an institution where art is not defined by virtue of being there, but rather, an institution of “past human behavior” and the “creative AI ecosystem” (Fraser; Burcaw, p. 63; Iervolino and Milne, p. 167). The major reason for this rational is also in how such AI artists’ work will fundamentally change as models get better, which, by effect, renders older works as history, in some sense of the word. Their removal from art galleries is because of their complicated relationship to the idea of *art*, particularly with the arguments of the excluded with especially labor theft, but some internal art world members, with kitsch.

<div class="parth">  
    <p> Conclusion </p>
</div>

Ultimately, the issue drawn, is that of AI art displayed in art galleries granting it legitimacy by virtue of occupying that space, and in so doing, building greater exclusion between the art world and its so-called outsiders, with criticisms, from both sides, of kitsch, style theft, and labor theft. Altogether, this reframes the tension of AI art as instead *should the art world’s institutions curate AI art*? This essay has answered that no, they should not, but rather, that history museums take on this tension, to revolutionize, for lack of a better word, themselves the same way art galleries did with the shift from institutional critique to institutionalized critique. By looking at the work of Gaskins, and its reception, it has been shown how, framed within institutionalized critique’s transformation of whatever is in the art gallery is *art*, that AI art engages a form of style and labor theft, wherein it is also receivable as kitsch. To that end, with the idea of kitsch holding more historical than art value, they therefore have more reason to be in history museums. In the end, the tension of AI art in the institutional world is one that has potential to create a new understanding of the history museum, as proposed here, and in so doing, evolve its very function much how art galleries evolved in function by institutionalizing critic.
