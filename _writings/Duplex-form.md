### ENGL 2380 Reading Poetry | University of Guelph | Exploring the duplex form of poetry created by Jericho Brown

By Amir Benkelai

The duplex is a form created by Jericho Brown using the forms of blues, sonnet, and ghazal (Brown). Introduced in his book *The Tradition*, it features the strict couplet and repeating lines format from the ghazal, the themes of struggle from the blues, and volta as used by the sonnet, to name a few. Hoelzer, who explores the form from the historicity of the sonnet, introduces a reading of the duplex as one of not just black and queer identity, but also of “critical hope”, and how Brown challenges the white tradition through historic evidence of Black poetics (Hoelzer). The essence of the form in defining Black/queer identity as Hoelzer explores remains in the work of others who have shifted the duplex’s elemental makeup, for instance Antoinette Brim-Bell’s *Duplex: Black Mamas Praying*. In comparing how two differing methods of writing the duplex, the idea of “critical hope”, a new perspective on the attitude of negativity within poetics, can be explored (Hoelzer). Hence, this short comparative essay, in agreement with Hoelzer’s reading, shall explore “critical hope” from how the Duplex is a form that communicates experience and empathy to its readers, through Brown and Brim-Bell’s works. 

In *Duplex (The opposite of…)*, Brown appears to discuss the experience of rape from a queer lens. The first couplet—taken from how the ghazal is a poem made of couplets— is loosely iambic pentameter, thus not leaning into the sonnet’s strict rhythm, and repeats line 2 in line 3 (Brown; Brown, lines 2-3). Then, the following couplet, as per the ghazal format, begins with parts of line 2. As commented upon by Hoelzer, these two beginning couplets showcase the concept of how Black poetics blend Western and Black traditions together (Hoelzer). For instance, the discussion of struggle and sex combined with ideas of love are a combination of blues and sonnets, who deal with each respective theme (Brown; Academy of American Poets). This allows for an empathetic reading of rape within Black and/or queer communities as there is no “filter” to the message produced. Taking the sonnet’s heavy use of metaphoric language, “The opposite of rape is understanding” introduces a challenging thought to the reader, where the tenor is “rape”, and the vehicle is “understanding” (Brown, line 1). Here, the speaker appears to define “understanding” as an antonym to “rape”, where they may be critiquing how “rapeoes not exist in a vacuum—as Hoelzer comments on Brown’s form as a critique of “white tradition”— but is instead “created” by circumstances, or “understanding” (Brown, line 1; Hoelzer). 

Through the volta—a traditional element of the sonnet, here explicitly displayed as each new couplet after the indented one introduce a new idea— this idea is expanded upon by the speaker’s own experience of such reality. In this new couplet, there is evidence for the speaker experiencing such an incident, in the last three words of the line “Men roam shirtless as if none ever hurt” (Brown, line 6). Proceeded with “Men roam in that myth. In truth, one hurt me.”, repeating the previous line but shifting “…shirtless as if….” to “…that myth.” (Brown, line 6). Here, the experience of rape by the speaker is made plain, a key of the blues form (Academy of American Poets). The sonnet’s traditional focus on love then highlights this plain revelation as one of conflict. Together, the reader is then allowed a form of “intimate access”, as explored by Hoelzer, through which empathy becomes generated. A form of “critical hope”, which can be further understood now using Brim-Bell’s piece (Hoelzer).
	

Brim-Bell’s *Duplex: Black Mamas Praying* shifts the form, with the obvious change of forgoing the indent. Instead in its place is a heavy use of dashes, almost like Emily Dickinson’s style. This serves as another instance of white poetics being transformed by Black poets (Hoelzer). This deviation allows the reader to slow down even more than in the original duplex form as used by Brown, since dashes naturally tend to bring a reader to a slower pace, especially when they surround specific words, almost as if highlighting them. Consequently, the volta then occurs less frequently, thereby absorbing itself further into the sonnet tradition—since it occurs twice now compared to after every indent— whilst maintaining parts of the ghazal and blues forms (Brown; Academy of American Poets). Exploring more despair than struggle, death is the topic of discussion here compared to Brown’s discussion of rape. The disparity of content between Brim-Bell and Brown may act as a display of which “version” of duplex may be best depending on which theme from the blues is being explored. In the framework of Hoelzer’s “critical hope”, Brim-Bell appears to be criticizing the popular racist line “the world don’t need you” in a means aiming to provide hope for the future (Hoelzer; Brim-Bell lines 2-3). Featuring it in the first two couplets, once as part of a line, another as dialogue, it stands in for how Black poems have historically shifted the Western tradition, and thus are able to diversify and add more to the American cannon, yet the cannon has remained whiter than ever (Hoelzer). Brim-Bell explores the notion that “the world does need you” metaphorically, fighting the despair of the original version (Brim-Bell lines 2-3). This is evident in proceeding couplets, for example the third one being the first volta where italics are introduced, which may be the speaker “casting” the prayer, and the repeating words of “may live” allude to the idea of “the world does need you” (Brim Bell lines 3, 10-11). 

Henceforth, through pairing the two duplex poems with a shift in the form, the blend of Western and Black poetics produces an intimate connection between Black/queer folk and the reader (Brown; Hoelzer). Then, through the sonnet’s metaphorical nature and the blues plain stating of struggle, sex, and/or despair, all together produces an exploration of Hoelzer’s “critical hope” idea, framing the struggles and despair of Black/queer communities as one of not necessarily negativity only, but also one of compassion and hope, the latter highlighted by the form itself (Academy of American Poets; Hoelzer).
