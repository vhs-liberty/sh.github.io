By Amir Benkelai

Frederick Douglass, in both his *Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave and My Bondage and My Freedom*, but specifically *My Bondage*, explores the space occupied by African religions or cosmologies in the African diaspora created by the slavery institution. He does so in consideration of the Christian perspective held by the white antebellum: that African beliefs are the devil’s trade (Brooks, 189). The depth of exploration done here by the texts create different interpretations of the slavery institution’s violence through the fight with Covey. Hence, this essay shall explore how by embodying the Christian perspective on African cosmologies, Douglass becomes able to take on a white authority, and thereby better outline the violence of the slavery institute.

According to John Brooks, in their paper “Sandy’s Root, Douglass’s *Mêtis*: ‘Black Art’ and the Craft of Resistance in the Slave Narratives of Frederick Douglass”, African religions were perceived as “…little more than superstition, witchcraft, or apocryphal barbarism”, a perception Douglass takes on when describing his reaction to the root knowledge of Sandy Jenkins in *My Bondage* (Brooks, 190). He calls it “very absurd and ridiculous, if not positively sinful” and “dealings with the devil” where he draws from the Christian faith (Douglass, My Bondage). That is, he is drawing on religious rhetoric by calling the root idea “sinful” and the work of the devil. This is in line with how the white antebellum viewed these religions but is also very different to how in the *Narrative*, he simply describes his reaction as having “rejected the idea” (Douglass, Narrative 136). Two reasons may be attributed to why Douglass goes from simply rejecting, to rejecting using Christian rhetoric. The first is that he is focalizing his narrative through the lens of the white antebellum because he is citing sin and devil dealing, which consequentially, then, posits him as a white authority. This is a use of ethos to ground himself in a position where he generates a tone that to the intended audience is perceived as objective and neutral. Brooks highlights this in their analysis of Douglass’s remark on Sandy having “good sense”, where they similarly define it as “Douglass adopt[ing] the subject position of white authority” because that is the language of the white antebellum (Brooks 192).

The second is what Brooks refers to as the “illegibility of Yorùbá folk knowledge” where Douglass does not dismiss Sandy’s knowledge and instead empowers it (Brooks 191). The way he does this is by characterizing Sandy as “good-hearted”, “famous among the slaves of the neighborhood for his good nature” and a man of “good sense”, which positions the reader to see him as an enslaved individual with a wholehearted reputation in the region (Douglass, My Bondage). Thus, Douglass maintains his ethos because he “disarms dismissive readers” and in doing so leaves room for the reader to think about Sandy’s knowledge (Brooks 192). This space of thinking is done to legitimatize African folk knowledge as Sandy states that book learning had not helped Douglass’s case, where Douglass comments that the argument was powerful at that moment (Douglass, My Bondage). In doing so, he grants reason to Sandy, which draws away slightly from his ethos as a white authority since the white antebellum would never do such a thing. Yet, this then allows Douglass to give power to African folk wisdom, which as Brooks states, allows Douglass to “disrupt the ideological order of enslavement”, colloquially meaning challenging the Western knowledge infrastructure (Brooks 193).

Thus, Douglass shifts from simply rejecting to rejecting through Christian rhetoric because it allows him to position himself as a white authority and to strengthen African folk knowledge. This then affects how he goes on to describing his fight with Covey as he suddenly finds the strength to resist and then never gets whipped again. It may be that this strength comes from the root itself because of its resistive nature. That is, as the root’s mystical property is definitive non-Western knowledge, it may symbolize his strength because the persistence of African folk knowledge in the diaspora is a form of resisting, and thereby attainment of strength, against the slavery institute (Brooks 197). Here, then, Douglass is not outright rejecting the possibility of it working, but by appearing to reject this possibility, he returns to his ethos of a white authority, a believer of Christianity who cannot accept such “witchcraft” (Brooks 190). However, as was previously mentioned, this root continues to be significant in his narratives because it challenges the slavery institute by going against the Western knowledge infrastructure. The reason for this is defined by Brooks as a way to “raise questions about the devices of racial legitimation in general, and the institution of slavery in particular” which *My Bondage* further touches on in its styling choices (Brooks 196). That is, the choice to capitalize “I WAS A MAN NOW” and “A FREEMAN” when in the *Narrative* these are written as “my own manhood” and “a determination to be free” respectively (Douglass, My Bondage; Douglass, Narrative 137). One is exclamatory and jumps at the reader whilst the other is less so making them far more noticeable for the reader because it reminds them that this is a text about an enslaved individual by an enslaved individual. This is because, previously, he had posited himself as a white authority, as was explored above, and now he is bringing the reader back to the fact that he is still enslaved. Except, he is now a “FREEMAN” because he was never whipped again, though in the *Narrative* he adds he only had four years left, a detail seemingly left out in *My Bondage*.

What this all means for the violence of the slavery institute is found in the difference of the fight’s details in both narratives. Particularly, he introduces in *My Bondage* that the fight had gone to the “cow yard”, gives voice to Bill, and introduces Caroline (Douglass, My Bondage). The addition of Caroline and the detail that the fight went into another space, the cow yard, add detail to the *Narrative*’s simple statement that the fight went on for two hours (Douglass, Narrative 137). This has two main impacts on how a reader may perceive the fight between the narratives. First, there is more violence because Douglass is now using words like “rebellion” and “cowardly” to describe the fight; he moves away from his ethos to render Covey as a coward which shocks the white antebellum reader yet is a statement on how the slavery institute is boldened by such cowardice (Douglass, My Bondage). Douglass extends this idea in *My Bondage* not just by the word choice but also in how Caroline is later punished for her disobedience. This, then, is the second perception, where an enslaved individual is revolting against an enslaver, and the enslaver, who is portrayed as “cowardly”, never lays hand on the instigator, but does lay hand on Caroline, another enslaved individual and instigator (Douglass, My Bondage). In doing so, he is re-focalizing his narration slightly to fit the mold of a white antebellum perspective because an enslaved individual is being punished for instigating.

This very difference then allows for the capitalization to be even starker, as it may now act as an exclamation of victory, or him solidifying how he “should have been victorious” had Covey not simply given up (Douglass, My Bondage). This is also because these capitalized words come near the conclusion of the fight, in the line where Douglass comments on winning his fight with Covey when that was clearly unexpected. The violence of this fight, inherently, is against the racial episteme where the enslaved is not meant to be able to go unpunished for resisting. Truly, it is maintained in Coraline’s case, but not Douglass’s.

In trying to understand this, Douglass explains that it may have to do with how Covey wants to maintain his reputation as a “Negro breaker” but also poses the possibility of the root (Douglass, My Bondage; Douglass, Narrative 138). Previously, it was explored how the root may be resistive in nature, and how he posits himself as a white authority by responding to it as not working. However, now the root expands into a question of how this very resistance and declaration of becoming “a freeman” position themselves within the broader racial episteme (Douglass, Narrative 138). This is explored by finding how the concept of *mêtis* relates to this episteme. Brooks come to define “book learning” as “the rationalized presumptions of the racial episteme” and Sandy’s “good sense” as “a reaction to racialization and enslavement” which describes the two different forms of knowledge as reactionary to one another (Brooks 197). That is, for example, when Douglass resists Covey, he states that he is “heedless of consequences”, which means he has made use of his knowledge to instigate instead of continuing to fall in the racial episteme where he simply submits to being whipped (Douglass, My Bondage). This then showcases that in the slavery institute, violence is embedded within the racial episteme, which once challenged through *mêtis* or “opportunistic and resourceful knowledge”, like Douglass did here, it turns to a form of resistance not just of the institute, but of the very knowledge it is comprised of (Brooks 196).

The knowledge that, inherently, posits the Western—and to that end Christian— views as dominantly correct is put on a chopping block as Douglass maneuvers his ethos to bring the reader to see various parts of the violence in the racial episteme. For Brooks, this is a “strategy of aesthetic resistance because it can implant uncertainty in the minds of the antebellum white readers” which is, at the end, what Douglass aims to achieve through his writing (Brooks 200).

Therefore, coming to the end of this essay, Douglass has created an ethos for himself where he focalizes through the white antebellum and shifts back to an enslaved focalization to disarm and confuse the reader, whereby he becomes better able to resist, and decipher the slavery institution’s violence to his targeted readers of the South.
