By Amir Benkelai

African Americans communicate in a dialect of English distinct from American English (Rickford 315-316). This speech, known as the African American English Vernacular, or AAEV for short, is a creole language developed by enslaved folks brought to the British colonies in the Americas from West Africa (Rickford 317). It finds itself often debated on grounds of validity, despite its significance as a form of African American resistance (Rickford 315-316). Jordan Peele’s film *Get Out*, through the protagonist Chris, helps shine a new perspective on this matter. That is in how when Chris speaks AAEV with fellow African Americans he is responded to in standard English rather than AAEV (*Get Out* 38:41; 44:59). In so doing, Peele turns this very speech into a foreshadowing tool, and Chris, then, too comes to realize why none of the other African Americans use AAVE. This essay, therefore, will briefly explore the historical development of AAVE to frame how Peele turns it into a foreshadowing tool that also acts as a means of fetishizing Black bodies.

In John R. Rickford’s 1997 paper “Prior creolization of African American English? Sociohistorical and textual evidence from the 17th and 18th centuries,” he explores AAVE within the early American colonies (Rickford 315-316). In the New England and Middle Colonies, there is more evidence pointing to this dialect being imported from the Caribbean rather than developed within them (Rickford 318-322). This is in part because of population differences, where the Caribbean had a larger proportion of enslaved folks (Rickford 321-322). These colonies, in contrast, had lower such populations compared to Whites (Rickford 321-322). Rickford states this as “while the likelihood that pidginization and creolization took place in the Middle Colonies is very low, the likelihood that pidgin or creole speech entered these colonies from *the Caribbean* is very high” (Rickford 322). The evidence for this is from compilations of data from surveys and writings in the 1700s, such as documented number of imported enslaved folks and writings of what and how they spoke (Rickford 316-320). Interestingly, the situation differs somewhat in New York. Here, Rickford explains that “the relative cultural and social autonomy of 18th century New York Blacks, noted by Dillard, might also have helped to spread and preserve creole speech” (Rickford 322). This is based on a survey that showed enslaved individuals as twenty-one percent of the region’s population in 1738, where they had the opportunity to meet and socialize despite laws against it (Rickford 322).

Turning to the Southern Colonies, because of the development of the cultivation enslavement economy, there is more likelihood of this creole speech developing internally (Rickford 322-323). This is especially so because of higher enslaved populations in contrast to Whites, which culminated in greater segregation, and in some places, led to major pidginization and creolization (Rickford 323). For example, in the Sea Islands, with the creole language Gullah, still practiced today, given that “Black proportions were as high as 79 percent in some parishes and even higher (93%) on individual islands and plantations” (Rickford 323). In Peele’s *Get Out*, this colonial history of the development of the creole language of AAVE becomes portrayed by the aesthetic of the estate, and the non-AAVE speech of other African American characters.

In the scene beginning at 38 minutes and 41 seconds of the film and ending at 40 minutes and 19 seconds, Chris goes to speak to the groundskeeper Walter (*Get Out* 38:41). Chris prompts him by saying “What’s up man, they working you good out here, huh?” to which the groundskeeper responds, “Nothing I don’t want to be doing” (*Get Out* 38:43; 38:46; 38:57).  The tone Chris takes on, and the shortening of the words, is indicative of the creole language. Yet, as the camera shot of his face shows at 39 minutes and 1 second of the film, he is confused by Walter’s standard English response (*Get Out* 39:01). This is not something Chris alone detects, but any viewer of the film with knowledge of how African Americans communicate with each other. The communication continues in standard English, with the absence of AAVE creating an uncanny effect, more so due to Walter’s manner of pronunciation. He is revealed as Rose’s grandfather later near the end of the film, which serves as an explanation as to why he does not speak in AAVE. Not only, but Chris’s statement of “working you good out here” may be connotative of African American brotherhood because it indirectly refers to the White employer, wherein there is a callback to the colonial history given the aesthetic of the estate (*Get Out* 38:43). In so doing, Chris, then, also expects someone to relate to, yet Walter’s reaction opposes that. This refusal of engaging AAVE, and the lack of care for African American brotherhood, becomes more apparent when looking at how Peele comes to showcase the fetishization of Black bodies in the film.

Julia Mollenthiel in “Horrifying Whiteness and Jordan Peele’s *Get Out*” explores the idea of “horrifying whiteness” (Mollenthiel 207-208). This ties to the idea of “white monstrosity,” when white individuals commit monstrous acts on Black people, for example, with the lynchings that happened across the United States (Mollenthiel 207-208). Mollenthiel says that “Peele transfers the gaze typically directed at Blackness to horrifying whiteness which exposes white monstrosity, and in turn, illuminates Black vulnerability” (Mollenthiel 208). That is, how White people are often portrayed as the hero where Black people are the villain allowing for the continuation of the idea of White as good and Black as evil (Mollenthiel 208-209). This idea then allows for the continued “monsterization of Black people and the endorsement of white terrorism” (Mollenthiel 209). It excuses terrific acts by White people because it becomes portrayed as saving, as something else. Mollenthiel goes on to identify this as the “white gaze,” that is the camera gives the white perspective, sidelining completely the Black one (Mollenthiel 209). This, historically, has been the case for horror cinema, yet Peele comes to switch this by putting the White family of the Armigates as the villains (Mollenthiel 209-210). He displays the horrifying whiteness from a Black perspective (Mollenthiel 210). This enhances the effect of objectification as now, since the viewer is posited through Chris’s perspective, they too are part of the objectification. In so doing, AAEV becomes more than a foreshadowing tool for the atrocities of later, it also now offers insight into how Black bodies get fetishized without an understanding of the cultural nuances.

At 15 minutes 39 seconds, when Chris first meets the family, Rose’s father, Dean, asks him, “how long has this been going on…this…this…thang,” where he uses the AAEV word “thang” instead of “thing” (*Get Out* 15:41). Here, Dean uses the vernacular to connect with Chris, to show a form of understanding. In so doing, he then also may be using the vernacular to showcase that he does not see it as inferior, that he does in fact respect African Americans, but overall, the effect generated is not this. Chris shows some discomfort in the scene, and especially so as the connotation of “thing” here is the relationship. It is turning it into a “thing,” where the combination of standard English with AAVE itself may be objectifying the interracial nature of the relationship. In so doing, then, this turns into a fetishization of the Black body, as it contrasts Black with white, which, to borrow from Mollenthial, “echoes the sexual myth about Black men” (Mollenthiel 215).  This speech too then is the only instance of AAVE being used by White people, and thereby, even the African Americans depicted elsewhere in the film. It overall comes to be the first moment of foreshadowing and unsettling othering that Chris comes to face before the family meetup.

During the scene of the family meetup is the other instance of AAVE appropriation, this time with Logan King, who is an African American person kidnapped and forced into the procedure in the beginning of the film (*Get Out* 02:42). The interaction begins at 44 minutes 59 seconds and ends at 46 minutes 4 seconds. Chris, smiling, approaches Logan saying, “Good to see another brother around here,” to which Logan responds, “hi…yes, of course it is,” then, “something wrong” (*Get Out* 44:59; 45:04; 45:11). The camera shifts to Chris’s face which quickly changes to confusion, as of course, this is an unusual response. The tone and manner of speaking echoes that of the white elite. Mollenthial identifies this too in the body language of the African American dap, where “Logan’s offering of a hand shake instead of the dap signifies that he has been whitewashed” (Mollenthial 216). That is, it casts to the viewer, and to Chris, that something is not right. It signals the disconnect of vernaculars, calling back to the colonial history of AAVE, how it was a language between the enslaved separate of the White people (Rickford 324).

Coming to the end of this essay, it has been shown that AAVE began within the Caribbean and was imported into the American colonies, from where it further evolved as a creole language. Its colonial history then highlights how AAVE is a means of communication between African Americans, and as such, in Peele’s *Get Out*, its absence in places it should be foreshadow later events. Here, then, it also comes to fetishize the Black body, as it posits it as an object irrespective of culture, drawing further into the colonial history and objectification of African Americans.
