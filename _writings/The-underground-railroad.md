### ENGL 3940 ... | University of Guelph | Assignment Description here

By Amir Benkelai

Freedom is one of those words whose definition and very construct can differ between person-to-person and/or group-to-group. In Colson Whitehead’s *The Underground Railroad*, the movement of Cora from the plantation in Georgia, to South Carolina, then the attic in North Carolina, followed with her recapturing journey through Tennessee, from where she is rescued and ends up at a farm in Indiana, to finally finding freedom westward, all produce two definitions of freedom within the context of Black liberation. The first is owning and defending whereas the second adds the ability to live without fear. An example of the first is in the plantation in Georgia, where freedom is defined by the garden plot kept by Cora, her mother before her, and Cora’s grandmother. This is first seen in Cora’s decisions “to hold on to her stake” and fight “for the dirt” (Whitehead, pp.14-15). An example of the second is in South Carolina, where Cora begins first carving what freedom means for her. Despite its 21st century origins, such definitions of freedom can be found in prints of the 19th century. Joshua McCarter Simpson’s poem “The Song of the ‘Aliened American’” as published in *The Emancipation Car* will hence be used to see how well Whitehead captures 19th century understandings of Black liberation.

Cora is how the reader comes to view freedom contextualised by Black liberation, itself composed of Black geographies, meaning the movement of Black people across the diaspora (Waller, pp. 45-46; Bledsoe and Wright, p. 420). This then posits her as a means for the reader to understand freedom from the lens of Black liberation, where in so doing, she also becomes an embodiment of freedom itself as an idea. Since she is moving throughout the novel, so too then is freedom, or in other words, freedom is in transit. This is specifically in reference to the second definition, that of owning, defending, and being able to live without fear, where the ability to live without fear will be understood as composed of dimensions. Altogether, as identified by others like Nicole Waller in “Marronage or Underground? The Black Geographies of Colson Whitehead’s *The Underground Railroad* and Ta-Nehisi Coates’s *The Water Dancer*,” Cora will be used to read freedom as echoing the ideas of Black geographies and Black spatial politics. Here, the developments of the 19th century will be seen through maroon settlements and what Simpson’s poem infers. In doing so, the propagation of these ideas will be explored by looking at how Black liberation operated in the 20th century, using Adam Bledsoe and Willie Jamaal Wright’s article “The Pluralities of Black Geographies.” This essay thus argues that Cora in *The Underground Railroad* by Colson Whitehead is an embodiment of freedom, where freedom itself is an idea in transit, and that by looking at Simpson’s piece, and comparing historical developments of Black geographies, liberation, and spatial politics, it will be shown that Whitehead accurately portrays one construction of freedom within Black liberation.

1.Definition One: Freedom as Owning and Defending


Waller identifies the garden as where “Cora asserts her space” adding later that it is a means to “outline the plantation landscape.” (Waller, p. 52). She also adds that “as a system, the plantation functions as the blueprint of a violent anti-Blackness” (Waller, p.52). Indeed, the garden was a means of control for Cora and may be considered as Black space because of its separation from the product of the plantation giving it a sort of autonomous structure. This echoes one of the functions of maroon settlements, or as another example, one of the Black Panthers’ tenants (Waller, pp.45-46; Bledsoe and Wright, pp. 426-427). In fact, the garden space as such an autonomous structure, or better yet, zone, politicises the landscape; a politicisation furthered by the split of north and south, followed with a southern takeover. Waller reads this as a “perceived difference between north and south” that “proves to be deceptive” (Waller, p. 53). Within the frame of the garden space, before the southern takeover, it was defended and used to expand communal relations. This is evident by its contribution of “turnips or greens to the birthday feasts” and the extensive defending the grandmother, mother, and then Cora engage (Whitehead, p.11). For example, digging “‘a hammer in they heads’” and the destruction of the doghouse in protest of Blake, culminating in cleaning up “the mess that had been made of her plot” (Whitehead, p.14; pp. 18-20). However, the existence of this zone is threatened by the existence of the south, since at the end, it is still all owned by the Randall Estate.

Waller interprets that the southern takeover of the northern half, where Cora resided, was a tainting “by violence” (Waller, p.53). Truly, the plantation becomes not just more violent, but that garden space becomes more difficult to care for. Its face as an autonomous zone and a greater politicisation of the anti-Black landscape slowly turns back to “the joke it was—a tiny square of dirt that had convinced her she owned something” (Whitehead, p.184). This is Cora’s reflection in the attic in North Carolina. It brings the reality that freedom as defined with the garden was not freedom at all; that freedom is not just owning and defending land, it is being able to live without fear, without violence. Here, living without fear is to also say the removal of the possibility of losing what one owns. These elements are most importantly what distinguishes maroon settlements and the tenants of the Black Panthers when defining the garden as a definitive Black space (Waller pp. 45-46; Bledsoe & Wright pp. 426-427).

Lara Langer Cohen, in the “Introduction” chapter to her book *Going Underground*, states that “literal undergrounds…were important to the emergent figurative meaning of the underground” (Cohen, p.6). The Underground Railroad, as it were, was never a real underground, yet some did interpret it as such (Cohen, p. 8). Whitehead takes from this and makes the Railroad physically real, with tunnels and everything carrying Black people to freedom. Waller interprets this authorial choice as a stripping of “many older connotations: the idea of an ordered and linear progress from Southern bondage to Northern freedom, the vision of the Underground Railroad as a symbol for a benevolent nation, and the notion that anti-Blackness is a thing of the past” (Waller p. 54). Indeed, making the Underground Railroad a real Black space of locomotive transport adds to what it means to be free. In addition to ownership and defence is the ability to move and create family. These ideas may be symbolized by such a Railroad, and to that end then, it can carry one to a place where the ideas are real. Moving and creating family compose parts of living without fear, existing within Black spatial politics. A century later, such an idea continued with Garveyism and the work of the Black Panthers (Bledsoe and Wright, pp. 424-425; pp. 426-427).

2.Definition Two: Freedom as Owning, Defending, and Living Without Fear

Cora’s movement in South Carolina refers to these ideals of Black liberation through the introduction of America’s eugenicist heritage. Cora by this point is shown by Whitehead to have begun a romantic relationship with Caesar, as can be hinted by the line “he took a sip of his beer, focusing on the black bottle and avoiding Cora’s eyes” (Whitehead, p. 127). As they get increasingly settled, eventually, they decide to stay in South Carolina. Thus far, this highlights the second definition of freedom: living without fear, in all its respects, and having the ability to own and defend. This differs from the first, where there was only owning and defending, but also that owning and defending had imposed limitations—the garden after all could at any day be taken over. That second definition becomes troubled once Cora realizes what the doctors are up to. She finds out what the lady meant by “they’re stealing my babies;” the birth control program intended to euthanize and control the Black population (Whitehead, pp. 125-126).

This definition of freedom is one that moves, but unlike the first, it maintains itself across Cora’s journey. Evidence of such consistency can best be shown by Cora’s thought that “freedom was a community laboring for something lovely and rare” (Whitehead, p. 278). Here, the first definition of owning and defending, of how the garden plot could be a Black space in an anti-Black space, is overthrown by the idea of “something lovely and rare” since no such thing exists on a plantation given its anti-Black space roots (Whitehead, p.278; Waller, p.52). Instead, the second definition is strengthened, since the elements of living without fear are absolute. This thought reflects the idea of living without repercussion, without possibility of losing what one owns, of creating family, and of moving all through the idea of “community” and “love” (Whitehead, p.278). This echoes maroon settlements, as observed by Waller, but also echoes later movements of Black liberation, such as with Garveyism. This is a later resurgence of Return to Africa, first seen with Britain’s Sierra Leone, then America’s Liberia (Bledsoe and Wright, pp. 424-425). Another example is grassroot communities, like with the Black Panthers Party (Bledsoe and Wright pp. 426-427). Yet, what this also shows then, from a Black spatial politics lens, is a question hidden in the novel, that of where can such freedom, as so defined, settle? Such a query, of course, naturally applies to Cora, not just because she embodies freedom as explained earlier, but also because she is looking for a place to settle.

3.A Comparison of Definition Two with Simpson’s “Song of the ‘Aliened American”

Thus far the accuracy of Whitehead’s portrayal has been through secondary articles. Now, Simpson’s “Song of the ‘Aliened American” will be used to showcase how the definition of freedom as owning, defending, and living without fear was one of 19th century Black liberation. Ownership is first purveyed in asking “Is there no one to plead / The black man’s cause” (Simpson, lines 16-17; line 28). The line “black man’s cause” directly refers to Black liberation evident by how the poem ends by proclaiming “Our chains must break” (Simpson, line 28). This can best be seen by looking at how defence comes up in asking “O! shall we longer bleed?” (Simpson, line 15). This is a reference to the beatings that occurred on plantations, examples of which are shown across the novel. It also, by extension, refers to violence against Black people outside plantations, like with the public hanging with Jamieson in North Carolina (Whitehead, pp. 162-163). To not bleed then is to defend oneself as with the plantation where Cora defends her plot against Blake (Whitehead, pp. 18-20). This idea too is found within the very idea of Black geographies, for example, with the militia developed to protect African Americans in the works of the Black Panthers Party, or The New Afrikans movement, both of late 20th century origins (Bledsoe and Wright, pp. 426-427; pp. 430-431). Together, it can be read that there is a flow of owning down to defending, most grounded by how the poem begins with descriptions of enslavement followed with calls of uprising. Living without fear, then, is seen within the lines “No! no! the time has come, / When we must not be dumb, / *We must awake*” (Simpson, lines 22-24). These lines echo those very tenants of what it means to live without fear, portrayed earlier on, where the first definition is, by a means, shadowed. It is to say no longer should ownership and defence be bound to the plantation, in an illusionary fashion, as discussed earlier. Rather, that it is time to act, to “awake,” meaning to move, to create family, to uprise, all evidenced by the proclamation of “our chains must break” (Simpson, line 24; line 28).

4.Conclusion: An Accurate Portrayal

The second definition thus constructed by Simpson’s 19th century poem, in conjunction with the historical movements and ideas of Black liberation brought up in this essay, all evidence that Whitehead develops an accurate portrayal of freedom with Cora, where then she is freedom’s embodiment. Between Georgia and South Carolina thereby exist two definitions of freedom, with one more maintained across the novel whilst the other exists for a moment. The one existing for a moment is that of the garden plot, which serves, if anything, to properly ground the second definition. This then is that of owning, defending, and living without fear, where living without fear is to live without repercussion, without possibility of losing what one owns, of creating family, and of moving. By the time Cora finds herself on Valentine’s farm in Indiana, she concludes to the idea of freedom as communal, wherein those very elements of life without fear appear. Here then is freedom positioned as something, that when in transit, must ask itself where to settle, given how many times that second definition is threatened in the novel. If anything, this definition and that question of where to settle are what Cora experiences across the novel. In so doing, Whitehead successfully echoes the various conceptions found in Black liberation movements, themselves interlinking with the ideas of Black geographies, where then exists Black spatial politics.
