### ENGL 2130 ... | University of Guelph | Assignment Description here

By Amir Benkelai

“The picture had fallen to the floor, uncovering the telescreen behind it” (Orwell, “TWO: X,” 197). This was the moment when Winston realized, in George Orwell’s *1984* novella, that he and Julia were not alone in the shop (Orwell, *1984*). This moment, and that very room across the novella, build a model of surveillance of being heard but not seen. Such a model is very much the surveillance of the digital age with the rise of surveillance capitalism, and in effect, big data (Holloway, 27-28). A place this takes hold in is the world of children, from toys suspected of transferring audio data to platforms like YouTube Kids (Smith and Shade, 1-2). In the novella itself are children, instead of being surveilled, turned into agents of surveillance (Orwell, *1984*). Children, therefore, within the scheme of surveillance, prove to be a worthwhile space to explore. Specifically, the place they are most commodified in, that of toys and the platforms they interact with. By looking at how children become agents of surveillance in *1984* and contrasting that with the surveillance model of heard but not seen, a surveillance capitalism framework of toys called The Orwellian Toy will be developed to understand the dichotomy of surveillance privacy.

Donell Holloway in “Surveillance capitalism and children’s data: the Internet of toys and things for children” explores briefly how children were and continue to take part in the capitalistic market (Holloway, 27-36). In the 21st century, this takes the form of surveillance capitalism, which “monetizes data obtained through surveillance” (Holloway, 28). It engages the child as not just a consumer, which can be “traced back to the 16th century where books, toys, and clothes were made for the children of wealthy bourgeoisie and nobility,” but also as actors of commodified data (Holloway, 30). It evolves from the child as the laborer, where “extremely low wages led families to supplement their house-hold income by sending their children off to work as well” (Holloway, 30). This evolution parallels the role of children in *1984*, where they are spies who snitch on their parents to the Thought Police, and in so doing, become the very surveillance today’s children are found in, only to then have children of their own upon becoming adults, who then spy on them, and so on (Orwell, *1984*). Not only, but this very act of spying is laborious in of itself, and as such, they are doing the labor of surveillance. This is first made apparent when Winston is looking for a spanner, where he goes to visit his neighbor, whose children were “dressed in the blue shorts, grey shirts, and red neckerchiefs which were the uniform of the Spies” (Orwell, “ONE: II,” 20). They also were playing as said spies, which made Winston get an “uneasy feeling” (Orwell, “ONE: II,” 20). This “uneasy feeling” may be a realization of the issues raised by being surveilled (Orwell, “ONE: II,” 20). Here, in juxtaposition to the current political economic scene of surveillance, the adults take the role of children, and the children take the role of the adults in terms of who is surveilled by who.

In surveillance capitalism, that is, children are only made available to Big Data—whose name echoes “Big Brother—” by their parents, with this process beginning at birth by how “expectant parents often announce their pregnancy by posting an ultrasound image of their foetus on Facebook, Instagram, and Pinterest” where they “include ultrasound images and other more detailed information about the unborn child” (Orwell, *1984*; Holloway, 29). Then, once born, their data is collected by “increasingly popular baby wearables, monitoring devices…about babies’ health, location or identity” and schools that add “further data which now supplements and far outweighs data collected from parental postings” (Holloway, 29). Such massed data continues with children’s toys and platforms like YouTube Kids, and with so much of their information existing online, many privacy risks are posed. This is very much what the children spying to the Thought Police allegorize, collecting data on their own parents, which are then traded if necessary; it is, returning to that scene with the neighbor, a “life of terror” (Orwell, *1984*; “ONE: II,” 21). Yet, this engages a surveilling model of seen and heard, since the children both see and hear their parents, whilst data collection usually involves hearing in the definition of “an instance of learning,” because what the data teaches is what children are saying, how they are playing, what they are interested in, etc. (“Hearing”).

Here then attention can be returned to that model developed earlier with the shopkeeper’s room, where near the end of Chapter 2, “the picture had fallen to the floor, uncovering the telescreen behind it” (Orwell, “TWO: X,” 197). This more accurately represents data collection, with the room itself allegorizing the child’s toy and online platforms. A survey by Karen Louise Smith and Leslie Regan Shade titled “Children’s digital playgrounds as data assemblages: Problematics of privacy, personalization, and promotional culture,” the datafication—meaning collection of data or turning into data— of children through the Fisher-Price Smart Toy and YouTube Kids is researched (Smith and Shade, 1-12). Looking more closely at the toy, Smith and Shade found that “some types of data are known to be collected because of a vulnerability report for the toy’s susceptibility to hacking produced by Stanislav, a cybersecurity manager at Rapid7” (Smith and Shade, 7). This act of datafication often includes many personal details submitted by the parent, for example, with the 2015 breach of Vtech’s Learning Lodge, “user profiles included profile photos, children’s names, as well as the month and day of their birth” including the “child’s relationship to adults…authorized to share messages” (Holloway, 33). These types of toys take part in what is called “the internet of toys (IoToys)” which find themselves in the realm of “the internet of things (IoT)” (Smith and Shade, 1). These are usually interconnected toys whose data collection is stored, and due to “databases” that “are often unsecured and easily hacked,” surveillance is easier than ever (Smith and Shade, 1; Holloway, 34). Entire “trading circles” exist that “redistribute stolen databases extensively for either financial gain or as a hobby” (Holloway, 34). This black market behavior is very much that room, where, day-to-day, children are heard, collected upon, but never really seen until they grow up where they discover just how much of their identity is online already—allegorically removing the picture from that wall.

This data collecting turned surveillance build many questions on privacy regulations (Holloway, 28). Not only, but their operational processes are very much what this essay seeks to define The Orwellian Toy as. That is, an object designed for children which comes to spy upon them, by collecting data, which often is sold to third parties for advertising, and if not, seems persistently susceptible to hacking, upon which, the data goes beyond the reach of the company (Smith and Shade; Holloway). In so doing, then, this data collection, containing sensitive information such as birthdate, height, photography, etc. all become means of hearing in terms of “an instance of learning” (“Hearing”). The “learning” of what children are up to is just like the children in *1984* who learn what their parents do and do not do to then snitch upon them, creating a “life of terror” (“Hearing;” Orwell, *1984*; “ONE: II,” 21). And in it all, this very framework of The Orwellian Toy is one of surveillance capitalism, of Big Data with the echo of “Big Brother,” who come to model that room where Winston, and later Julia, are heard—they are learnt about in terms of their traitorous intentions— but not seen (Orwell, *1984*). However, by technicality, the shopkeeper himself, who turns out to be part of the Thought Police, does see them go in and out, but is not necessarily seeing what they do (Orwell, *1984*). Hence why the model is very much of hearing and not looking, therefore, finding itself in the tradition of today’s capitalism surveillance.

In conclusion to this essay, The Orwellian Toy has been defined as the object spying on children through data collection containing sensitive information which is then sold and traded. They are put in the hands of Big Data, with its echo of “Big Brother,” by their parents, both before, and after, birth. This juxtaposes *1984*, where children surveille their parents, doing the labor of a spy by reporting what said parents do and do not do to the Thought Police, leaving them in a “life of terror” (Orwell, “ONE: II,” 21). Hence, the dichotomy of surveillance privacy is made raw, with many of its posing risks and issues, like those with the surveilling eyes of children in *1984*, awaiting to be answered in full.
